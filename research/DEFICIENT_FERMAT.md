# Residue classes forced by the divisor sum of a square

Let $n>0$ be a square and let $\ell$ be an odd prime with $\ell-1$ a power of two. If $\ell\mid\sigma(n)$, pick an exact component $r^e$ supplying $\ell$. Its exponent is even. The order of $r$ modulo $\ell$ divides both $e+1$ and $\ell-1$ unless $r\equiv1\pmod\ell$. The order is therefore one, giving $r\equiv1\pmod\ell$ and $\ell\mid e+1$. The prime-order case of Bang–Zsigmondy supplies a distinct prime $s$ with $\operatorname{ord}_s(r)=\ell$. The complete geometric sum $\sigma(r^e)$ is divisible by $s$, and $s\equiv1\pmod\ell$. [Primitive primes at prime-power length](../OddPerfect/Primitive.lean) · [Fermat-prime source and companion](../OddPerfect/DeficientThirdSource.lean)

For $3\sigma(n)=5n$, every such $s$ divides $n$ because an odd $\ell$ cannot divide $5-1$. Thus both ends of the directed order relation belong to the prime factors of $n$. A separate corollary at $\ell=3$ assumes directly that $5n$ is perfect and $3\mid\sigma(n)$. It also covers $5\mid n$, since a source one modulo three differs from five. Deriving perfection of $5n$ from $3\sigma(n)=5n$ by multiplicativity requires $5\nmid n$. [Projection to the prime factors of the deficient-perfect number](../OddPerfect/DeficientThirdSource.lean)

Suppose $n$ is an odd square, $3\mid n$, $3\sigma(n)=5n$, and at most two prime divisors of $n$ are one modulo three. Any exact source $r^e$ contributing two powers of three to $\sigma(n)$ would satisfy $9\mid e+1$ by lifting the exponent at $r\equiv1\pmod3$. The orders three and nine then require two distinct targets, both congruent to one modulo three and both different from $r$. This would give at least three prime divisors in that residue class. Hence each of at most two sources contributes at most one copy of three. From $v_3(n)=1+v_3(\sigma(n))\le3$ and evenness of $v_3(n)$, the exact component at three is $3^2$. Its divisor sum gives $13\mid n$. [Exact exponent and the forced prime 13](../OddPerfect/DeficientThreeExponent.lean)

Yang and Togbé’s 2023 classification proves that the only odd deficient-perfect number with four distinct prime factors is $3^2 7^2 11^2 13^2$. In §5.3 they list three candidates for the fourth prime $R$ at $p_2=17,p_3=23$ from $977\le R\le3517$ and $R\equiv1\pmod{510}$. The prime 3061 also meets both stated conditions. In that same case the authors established $n/d=3$ and hence $3\sigma(n)=5n$. The two-residue-prime argument here rules out the entire family: only $R$ among $3,17,23,R$ can be one modulo three. This supplies a proof independent of that enumeration. [Yang and Togbé, §5.3, p. 18](https://math.colgate.edu/~integers/x5/x5.pdf#page=18)

Pomerance uses related restrictions in his proof that an odd perfect number has at least seven distinct prime factors. His §1.11 forces distinct primes one modulo successive powers of the target prime when a source has the corresponding prime-power length. The square-divisor-sum lemma above applies before a perfection or deficiency equation is imposed. [Carl Pomerance, *Odd perfect numbers are divisible by at least seven distinct primes*, §1](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~carlp/PDF/paper1.pdf)

The Cui–Zhang–Li theorem gives $p_1=3$ and $p_2\le13$ for odd deficient-perfect numbers with four distinct prime factors. In the local proof the bound $p_2\le17$ leaves a single case, $p_2=17$. Then $3\sigma(n)=5n$ and $17\mid\sigma(n)$. The two companions modulo 17 exhaust the remaining prime factors $r,s$. Every prime one modulo 17 exceeds 52, so the strict abundancy bound is $(3/2)(17/16)(53/52)^2<5/3$, contradicting the defining equation. The Lean statement allows at most four distinct prime factors.
