# A non-Fermat prime divides every Euler index

If $N=q^a m^2$ is an odd perfect number in Euler form, then $i=\sigma(m^2)/q^a$ has a prime divisor $p$ for which $p-1$ is not a power of two. The theorem covers arbitrary prime multiplicities in $i$. Equivalently, even a product of powers of arbitrarily many Fermat primes cannot give the whole Euler index. For an arbitrary exact component, every positive power of a single Fermat prime is excluded as its index. [Formal statements](../OddPerfect/FermatIndexObstruction.lean)

For $a=1$, full Fermat support would make every even cofactor component send a divisor to $q$. A primitive divisor certifying a positive odd component length greater than one cannot occur at a prime whose predecessor is a power of two. The $q$-valuation of $\sigma(m^2)$ then gives $\omega(N)\le a+1=2$, incompatible with the checked lower bound $\omega(N)\ge5$. [Full-support count](../OddPerfect/FermatIndexSquareClass.lean) · [Distinct-prime lower bound](../OddPerfect/FewPrimes.lean)

For $a>1$, write $d=(a+1)/2$. Every prime $l\mid d$ divides $p-1$ for some prime $p$ with **odd** valuation in $i$. This follows directly from the checked product divisibility for the large odd-valuation index primes. The divisor $l$ is odd, so $p-1$ cannot be a power of two. Thus in this case a non-Fermat prime occurs already in the squarefree part of $i$. For a general exact component, every odd index identifies the Euler component. A putative index $2^j$ with $j\ge2$ contradicts that a nonspecial component index is 2 modulo 4, and an index of two has already been excluded. ## A prime outside the divisors of the Euler predecessor

For $a>1$ there is also a prime $p$ with odd valuation in $i$ such that $p\nmid q-1$. The proof needs only the return equation $\sigma(q^a)i=2m^2$, the Euler congruences, primality of $q$ and $m>0$. The predecessor conclusion and the non-Fermat conclusion above each assert the existence of a prime. The statements do not require that these primes coincide. [Formal return-equation theorem](../OddPerfect/FermatIndexObstruction.lean)

Put $A=(q^d-1)/(q-1)$ and $B=(q^d+1)/2$. These are coprime and $m^2=ABi$, so any odd valuation in either block also occurs with odd valuation in $i$. Choose an odd-valuation prime $r$ of the nonsquare $A$. If $r\mid q-1$, then $A\equiv d\pmod r$ forces $r\mid d$. A square $B$ would force $q\equiv1\pmod{16}$, and in that residue class the square half-sum requires $\gcd(d,q-1)=1$. Therefore $B$ is nonsquare. An odd-valuation prime $p$ of $B$ divides $q^d+1$ and cannot divide $q-1$, since $B$ is odd. [Half blocks and valuation transfer](../OddPerfect/EulerSquareClass.lean) · [Positive geometric nonsquare](../OddPerfect/GeometricNonSquare.lean)

The half-sum coprimality statement holds for every $q>1$ with $q\equiv1\pmod8$, odd $d$ and square $(q^d+1)/2$. A common prime $r$ of $d$ and $q-1$ is odd. Set $x=q^{d/r}$. The alternating block $(x^r+1)/(x+1)$ is nonsquare. Its only possible common prime with $x+1$ is $r$, while $x\equiv1\pmod r$ gives $r\nmid x+1$. This prevents $(x^r+1)/2$ from being a square. [Alternating-block nonsquare](../OddPerfect/AlternatingPell.lean) · [Coprime-block reduction](../OddPerfect/AlternatingBlock.lean)

Hirakawa's Theorem 1.1 concerns the square and prime-times-square shapes of the Euler index for $a>1$. The full-support conclusion here concerns the prime divisors of the index and also covers $a=1$. Its Lean proof uses the elementary restrictions above and the repository's matching result. [Hirakawa, primary paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.17084v3)
