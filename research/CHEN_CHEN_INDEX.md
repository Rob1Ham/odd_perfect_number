# Even prime powers as component indices

Chen and Chen's Theorem 1.1 applies to every exact prime-power component of an odd perfect integer. Write $q^a\parallel N$ and $i=\sigma(N/q^a)/q^a$. If $i=p^{2u}$ with $p$ prime and $u>0$, then both $\sigma(p^{2u})$ and $2u+1$ must be prime. The theorem therefore excludes every prime eighth power, since $8+1=9$. [Chen and Chen, *On the index of an odd perfect number*, Theorem 1.1, pp. 42 and 46](https://www.impan.pl/shop/en/publication/transaction/download/product/88031) · [Lean proof](../OddPerfect/ChenChenIndex.lean)

The formal statement `OddPerfect.chen_chen_even_prime_power_index` takes $q^a\mid N$ and $q^{a+1}\nmid N$ as separate hypotheses. It allows $p=2$ in its statement. The index identity $i\sigma(q^a)=2(N/q^a)$ excludes that case because $N/q^a$ is odd. An odd index identifies $q$ as the Euler prime. Its cofactor is consequently a square, $N/q^a=m^2$. Since the index is a square, the checked Gallardo exclusion at exponent greater than one forces $a=1$. [Gallardo's result and the Hirakawa proof](HIRAKAWA.md)

At exponent one, the source at $p$ has a nontrivial divisor sum which divides $qp^{2u}$ and is coprime to $p$. It therefore equals $q$. Its residue modulo $p$ is one, so $p\nmid q+1$. Taking $p$-adic valuations in the return identity $(q+1)p^{2u}=2m^2$ gives $2v_p(m)=2u$. Hence $q=\sigma(p^{2u})$. For every divisor $d>1$ of $2u+1$, the sum of the first $d$ powers of $p$ divides $q$. These sums increase strictly with their length, so $2u+1$ cannot have a proper nontrivial divisor. [Exact exponent-one identity](../OddPerfect/IndexPrimeReturn.lean)

Chen and Chen's Problem 1.2 asks whether there exists one odd prime length for which the geometric sum is composite at **every** prime base. The formal result is a necessary condition at the given prime base. The paper's separate Theorem 1.3 estimates index multiplicities using the number of distinct primes of $N$. Its Corollary 1.4 adds nineteen symbolic exclusions to the eleven previously obtained for the Euler index. Those conclusions require additional arguments, including bounds on that prime count and Diophantine restrictions on pure prime-power divisor sums. [Chen and Chen, pp. 42–48](https://www.impan.pl/shop/en/publication/transaction/download/product/88031)
