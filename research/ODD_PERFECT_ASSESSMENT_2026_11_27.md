# How close is this project to resolving odd perfect numbers?

**Research assessment · November 27, 2026 · local working tree through Wave K**

The repository can rule out entire families of odd perfect numbers and answer several related questions. An independent proof of the main conjecture still looks distant. Its strongest general reductions need either a bound that holds at every rank or an argument that forces a contradiction at arbitrary rank. Neither ingredient appears in the public imports. The best immediate opportunity I found is to couple the exact prime factors forced by two consecutive divisor sums to the number of times each prime can occur. This review supplies a Lean proof for the first step and a numerical example that rules out the simplest way of finishing it.

## What was verified

The target is the existence of an odd natural number whose proper divisors sum to the number itself. Equivalently, its full divisor sum satisfies $\sigma(N)=2N$. A positive solution requires a witness. A negative solution amounts to proving that every perfect number is even. Public references continue to list existence as open. The project owner reports checking an unpublished Lean resolution elsewhere. I could inspect the local project and public literature. The external proof's statement, hypotheses, and dependencies were unavailable, so the forecast below concerns independent discovery here. ## What the mathematics covers

Euler form writes every hypothetical candidate as $N=q^a m^2$, with $q$ prime, $q\nmid m$ and $q\equiv a\equiv1\pmod4$. Write $\omega$ for the number of distinct prime factors and $\Omega$ for the count with multiplicity. For an exact component $p^e\parallel N$, set $i_p=\sigma(N/p^e)/p^e$. ## Why the current counts can keep growing

There is a way to test the latest sixth-power-free counting argument at *all* scales. Give the Wave K count formulas an integer parameter $t\ge221$. Assign $t$ square sources to the class $1\bmod5$, all with two incoming sources, and $t$ quartic sources to the class $1\bmod3$, each with two incoming sources contributing four copies in total. Set the complementary source classes to zero. Including the Euler prime gives $\omega=2t+1$, $\Omega=6t+1$. In the repository's notation the total number of distinct incoming sources beyond the first is $E=2t$, and repeated copies within individual sources total $M=2t$.

Substitution satisfies the displayed cardinality and excess equalities and inequalities in the sixth-power-free clause of the Wave K master theorem, including $2\omega\le11t+11$. It also satisfies $\omega\ge443$ and $\Omega\ge1179$. This is an unbounded family of **count assignments**. Constructing primes with these assignments would require the exact divisor sums, which this test deliberately omits. A proposed counting proof needs a further inequality that excludes this family. The arithmetic of the actual source and target primes is one place to look. [Wave K clause](../OddPerfect/GlobalTier2WaveKObstruction.lean)

## A new local exclusion with a Lean proof

Suppose $N$ is sixth-power-free, $w$ is a prime of exponent four, and its divisor sum $s=\Phi_5(w)=w^4+w^3+w^2+w+1$ is a prime of exact exponent two in $N$. The existing argument shows that $\sigma(s^2)=\Phi_3(s)$ is composite. **The companion proves it has two distinct prime divisors, each occurring to odd valuation.** In particular, this chain cannot send its entire output to a single prime, or to one odd-valuation prime times a square. The precise Lean statement even allows any prime $w\mid N$ for which the displayed equality and the assumptions on $s$ hold. [Existing argument](../OddPerfect/SixthPowerFreePrimeCompositeSplit.lean) · [Checked arithmetic and odd-perfect corollary](QuarticChainCheck.lean)

Here is why the distinction holds. The existing factorization gives $\Phi_3(\Phi_5(w))=AB$ with
$$
A=w^2-w+1,\qquad B=w^6+3w^5+5w^4+6w^3+7w^2+6w+3.
$$
Modulo $A$ the second factor equals $5w-6$, and $25A=(5w-6)(5w+1)+31$. Thus $\gcd(A,B)\mid31$. In a sixth-power-free candidate, $31\nmid N$, while $\sigma(s^2)$ is odd and divides $N$. The two factors are therefore coprime.

The factor $A$ lies strictly between $(w-1)^2$ and $w^2$. For $B$, put $P=16w^3+24w^2+22w+15$. Then $256B-P^2=588w^2+876w+543$, which is positive and smaller than $2P+1$ for $w\ge19$. Consequently $P^2<256B<(P+1)^2$. This makes $B$ a nonsquare as well. Each factor contributes an odd-valuation prime. Coprimality makes the two primes distinct. The companion adds this theorem to the project as a standalone check. I have not established priority in the literature.

**How far it propagates.** For $k$ distinct intermediate primes $s$ of this kind, there are at least $2k$ distinct ordered source–target pairs. The Euler prime has total incoming valuation one, so at least $2k-1$ pairs terminate at other primes when $k\ge1$. These targets are $1\bmod3$ and cannot be exponent-two sources because that would force $3\mid N$. They are quartic, with capacity four each. If $D$ counts the distinct quartic targets among these pairs, $4D\ge2k-1$. Each receives another odd-valuation contribution by parity, which could come from another one of the same $k$ sources. This counting consequence is a deduction on paper from the checked local theorem and the repository's accounting. Measuring its improvement over existing inequalities needs the joint count. [Incoming valuations](../OddPerfect/PrimeByPrimeMultiplicityConservation.lean) · [Prime routing](../OddPerfect/SixthPowerFreeBipartiteParity.lean)

**A control that prevents an invalid proof.** Two distinct prime inputs $w=4153$ and $22639$ give prime outputs $s=297544762954421$ and $262692783167161121$. Both resulting $s^2+s+1$ have 73 to exact exponent one. Their residues modulo $73^2$ are 5183 and 73, respectively. Neither divisor sum has a prime factor below 53. These are local chains, without a claim that either occurs in a perfect number. They show why a proof needs to bound *reuse* of targets. Even the local small-prime exclusion permits reuse. The primality checks and residue calculations were performed separately from the Lean proof.

## Two more directions with explicit tests

**1. Extend the linear Euler analysis to one shared prime.** Let $H=(q+1)/2$, $I=i_q$, and $g=\gcd(H,I)$. Since $HI=m^2$, write $H=gh^2$, $I=gu^2$, $m=ghu$ with $\gcd(h,u)=1$. This follows prime by prime by subtracting the smaller valuation. In general $g$ may share primes with $hu$, so multiplicativity of $\sigma$ requires care.

Start with $g=r$ prime and $v_r(m)=1$. Then $r,h,u$ are pairwise coprime. The unique source $r_0$ of $q$ divides $I$ by an existing theorem. It cannot be $r$, since $r$ has exponent two in $N$ and divides $H$, which the existing square-source lemma forbids for a supplier of $q$. Hence $r_0\mid u$. With $W=\sigma(u^2)/q$, multiplicativity gives
$$
r u^2=\sigma(r^2)\sigma(h^2)W,\qquad \sigma(r^2)\mid u^2.
$$
The last divisibility uses $\gcd(r,\sigma(r^2))=1$. Thus every prime power in the divisor sum of the shared square component must divide $u^2$. This is a paper deduction that extends part of the coprime analysis to a genuine overlap. The proposed next theorem would show that, in this regime, a prime shared by two explicitly identified outputs is forced to exceed its available valuation, or would classify the cases that avoid that. Further cases include $v_r(m)=2$ and several shared primes. [Square-source exclusion](../OddPerfect/LinearEulerIteratedPeeling.lean) · [Unique supplier divides the index](../OddPerfect/LinearEulerTothObstruction.lean)

**2. Test whether a small component index can exist for structural reasons.** Set $C_p=e_p+\Omega(\sigma(p^{e_p}))$. The repository gives $C_p=1+\Omega(N)-\Omega(i_p)$ and $\sum_{p\mid N}C_p=2\Omega(N)+1$. If three different components had $\Omega(i_p)\le K$, these identities would give $3(1+\Omega(N)-K)\le2\Omega(N)+1$, hence $\Omega(N)\le3K-2$. At large multiplicity there are at most two possible witnesses for a fixed cap. Any proof based on averages should explain why one particular component captures almost all the factors. A practical first comparison is between the Euler component and its unique supplier on $a=1$. An example of the precise missing input is a fixed $K$ bounding the smaller of their two index multiplicities on that entire branch. Even such a theorem would leave $a>1$ and the effective residual exclusion. [Conservation](../OddPerfect/GlobalLogSlopePinch.lean) · [Exact global reductions](../OddPerfect/GlobalObstruction.lean)

My six-month planning judgment for an **independent** resolution on the present approach remains **low single digits**, consistent with the earlier 1–5% estimate. That range is subjective. It cannot be estimated statistically from theorem counts, lower bounds or the rate at which modules compile. I would revise it materially after a rank-independent contradiction on all of $a=1$, a uniform bound with a credible way to exhaust its residual cases, or access to a decisive verified lemma that applies across the uncovered branches. For the next research round, the two-target count is the best first investment because it has a checked starting point, an explicit obstruction to the naive argument and a clear test of whether the idea scales.
