# Component indices and reciprocal divisor sums

For an exact prime power `q^a` in a perfect number `N = q^a b`, define the component index `i = σ(b)/q^a`. Multiplicativity and the congruence `σ(q^a) ≡ 1 (mod q)` give the two integral identities `σ(b) = q^a i` and `σ(q^a) i = 2b`. Dris and Luca prove `i > 5` for every exact component of an odd perfect number in Theorem 1.1. Their Theorem 1.2 gives an effective upper bound for a hypothetical odd perfect number with bounded component index. [Dris and Luca, *A note on odd perfect numbers*, pp. 291–295](https://www.fq.math.ca/Papers1/54-4/DrisLuca09122016.pdf)

## Results checked in Lean

- `OddPerfect.exists_component_index` in `OddPerfect/Reciprocal.lean` constructs a positive index satisfying both identities and dividing `2N`. Its proof also applies to even perfect numbers.
- `OddPerfect.reciprocal_component_length` formalizes Dris–Luca Lemma 2.2. If `p` is an odd prime and `σ(p^λ) = q^β`, then `p^λ ∣ σ(q^a)` implies `p^(λ−1) ∣ a+1`. The proof computes the exact multiplicity of `p` in `q^β−1` and applies lifting the exponent.
- `OddPerfect.root_dvd_length_of_reciprocal_square` in `OddPerfect/EulerIndex.lean` aggregates that divisibility. If `m` is odd, `σ(m²) = q^β` and `m² ∣ σ(q^a)`, then `m ∣ a+1`. The exponent `β` may differ from `a`.
- `OddPerfect.euler_component_index_bound` proves `3q^a < 2m²` and gives an odd index at least three for the Euler decomposition `N = q^a m²`. If the index were one, the root bound would give `m ≤ a+1`. For `a ≥ 2`, the inequality `2(a+1)² < 5^a ≤ q^a` contradicts `q^a < σ(q^a) = 2m²`. The case `a=1` also fails. Dandapat, Hunsucker and Pomerance treated index one in greater generality in their Theorem 1. [Dandapat, Hunsucker and Pomerance, *Some new results on odd perfect numbers*, pp. 359–364](https://msp.org/pjm/1975/57-2/pjm-v57-n2-p07-p.pdf)
- `OddPerfect.component_index_parity_bound` in `OddPerfect/IndexTwo.lean` covers every odd perfect number, with the exact component and its index supplied as arguments. An odd exponent gives an odd index at least three. An even exponent gives index `2 (mod 4)` and index at least six. Thus every nonspecial component satisfies the inequality in Dris–Luca Theorem 1.1. The subsequent Euler argument treats indices three and five.

## How the nonspecial exclusion works

If the index is two, the identities become `σ(q^a) = b` and `σ(b) = 2q^a`. Deleting the selected nonspecial component from Euler's decomposition leaves `b = r^s m²` with `s` odd, `r` an odd prime coprime to `m`, and `m` odd. Write `s+1 = 2t`. The two factors of `σ(r^s)` are `r^t+1` and `1+r+⋯+r^(t−1)`. The first exceeds two and contributes a factor `q`. Any `q` dividing the second would divide both `r^t−1` and `r^t+1`, which is impossible. The second factor must therefore divide two, forcing `t=1`. The reusable lemma is `OddPerfect.exponent_one_of_sigma_dvd_twice_prime_pow`.

Now set `M=m²` and `U=(r+1)/2`. The equation `U σ(M)=q^a` makes `U>1` and `σ(M)` powers of `q`. Since `M ∣ σ(q^a)`, the reciprocal square lemma gives `m ∣ a+1`. From `σ(q^a)+M=2UM` one gets `q ∣ M+1`. Multiplying the identity by `q−1` gives `U ∣ (q−1)M−1`, so `U < qM` and `q^(a−1) < 2M²`. For `a ≥ 12` this contradicts `2(a+1)^4 < 3^(a−1)`. Consequently the odd `m` is at most eleven. Lean verifies `gcd(σ(m²),m²+1)=1` for those six values. This forces `σ(M)=1` and `M=1`, whereas the odd prime `q` would divide two. The arithmetic theorem `OddPerfect.not_index_two_with_euler_cofactor` takes just the displayed equations and structural conditions on the bases.

## Comparison with the published proof

Section 3 of Dris and Luca handles small indices together. For the branch `q>5`, the authors bound their product `M` by 200,000 and then invoke at least eight other prime factors, with at most one exceptional component. For `q≤5` they use a primitive prime divisor theorem and finish with congruences modulo nine and twenty-five. The Lean index-one and index-two arguments above use no lower bound on the size of an odd perfect number or its prime-factor count. [Dris and Luca, §3](https://www.fq.math.ca/Papers1/54-4/DrisLuca09122016.pdf)

The full-return hypothesis in Lemma 2.2 matters for extending the proof. From `σ(q^a) i = 2b`, an odd prime `p ∣ i` can lose powers when passing from `b` to `σ(q^a)`. A future use of the aggregated root lemma must establish that its square argument divides the returning divisor sum. The prime-index return lemma below explicitly handles that loss.


## Euler indices three and five

For either remaining index `i`, a component `r^e` of `m²` contributes the factor `i` in `σ(m²)=q^a i`. Its exponent is positive and even. For each odd prime power dividing `e+1`, the primitive-order theorem supplies a prime divisor of `σ(r^e)` having exactly that order. The modulus `i` cannot realize a nontrivial odd order because `i−1` is a power of two. Thus every such order occurs at `q`, so `e+1` is prime. The additional divisor `i` forces `r ≡ 1 (mod i)` and `e+1=i`. In particular `i ∣ q−1`. These local statements are `OddPerfect.prime_length_of_two_target_component` and `OddPerfect.exceptional_small_index_component` in `OddPerfect/SmallEulerIndex.lean`.

Write `m² = r^e v²` with `r ∤ v`. Then `σ(r^e)=iU`, `U σ(v²)=q^a`, and `σ(q^a) i = 2r^e v²`. Since `i ∣ m`, the last identity gives `i ∣ σ(q^a)`. Together with `q ≡ 1 (mod i)`, this gives `i ∣ a+1`. For every prime of `v` different from `i`, the full exponent returns. At `i`, if `i^c` exactly divides `v`, at least `2c−1` powers return. For `c≥2`, the adjusted LTE gives `i^(2c−2) ∣ a+1`. The case `c=1` uses `i ∣ a+1` directly. Hence `v ∣ a+1`. This is `OddPerfect.root_dvd_length_of_prime_index_return` in `OddPerfect/IndexReturn.lean`.

Set `M=v²`. Multiplication of the return identity by `(q−1)r` gives `ir q^(a+1) = 2M(q−1) r^(e+1)+ir`. Since `U ∣ q^a` and `r^(e+1) ≡ 1 (mod U)`, we have `U ∣ ir+2M(q−1)`. Together with `r² ≤ r^e < iU`, this gives `r ≤ 35qM`, `U ≤ 177qM` and `q^(a−1)<360M²`. Since `q≡1 (mod 4)` and `i ∣ q−1`, we have `q≥7`. For `a≥9`, the inequality `360(a+1)^4 < 7^(a−1)` contradicts `v ≤ a+1`. Thus `a∈{1,5}` and `v≤6`. As `v` is odd with `i ∣ v`, we get `v=i`. This leaves at most the three prime bases `q,r,i`, contrary to the locally proved four-prime lower bound. The arithmetic step is `OddPerfect.small_index_root_le_six` in `OddPerfect/IndexEstimate.lean`.

The resulting `OddPerfect.five_lt_divisor_sum_component_index` in `OddPerfect/DrisLuca.lean` states `5 < σ(N/q^a)/q^a` for every odd perfect `N` and positive exact prime-power component `q^a`. This reproduces Dris–Luca Theorem 1.1 using the four-prime bound and explicit divisor-sum arithmetic. It gives a necessary condition on hypothetical odd perfect numbers.

Hirakawa's Theorem 1.1 treats the Euler component specifically. For Euler exponent greater than one it excludes any index that is a square or a prime times a square. The exponent-one case has separate treatment in the paper. Its quadratic-order and generalized Fermat arguments are independent of the Lean proof here. [Hirakawa, 2023 preprint, published 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17084)

## Finiteness with bounded index

`OddPerfect.finite_of_bounded_component_index` in `OddPerfect/BoundedIndex.lean` proves Dris–Luca Theorem 1.2 for any real threshold `K`. Its integral counterpart `OddPerfect.le_of_bounded_component_index` gives the computable upper bound `OddPerfect.componentIndexBound K` when the index is at most the natural number `K`. The real-threshold theorem applies the integer bound at the natural ceiling. The bound can be very large. [Dris and Luca, Theorem 1.2 and §4](https://www.fq.math.ca/Papers1/54-4/DrisLuca09122016.pdf)

The support estimate `OddPerfect.prime_count_le_of_component_index` in `OddPerfect/IndexSupport.lean` gives `ω(N) ≤ 4 floor(log₂ i) + 4`. Write each cofactor component sum as a power of `q` times a part coprime to `q`. The product of all those coprime parts divides `i`, so at most `floor(log₂ i)` of them exceed one. At most another `floor(log₂ i)` distinct primes divide `i` itself. After removing these two sets and the selected and Euler primes, every remaining `p` has exponent `e≥2`, feeds only `q` and receives its full `p^e` back. Lemma 2.2 gives `p^(e−1) ∣ a+1`, including `p ∣ a+1` when `e=2`.

Let `t=ω(a+1)`. The cover just proved gives `ω(N) ≤ t + 2 floor(log₂ i) + 2`. The divisor-count product gives `2^t ≤ τ(a+1)`. The primitive-order result `OddPerfect.component_length_divisor_count` bounds that divisor count by `ω(N)`. As `2t ≤ 2^t` for every natural `t`, including zero, combining the two inequalities gives the claimed uniform prime-count bound. The effective version of Dickson's theorem in `OddPerfect/Dickson.lean` converts it into a bound for `N`. This proof of finiteness does not require solving a family of Diophantine equations.

## Prime factorization of the index

For an Euler component the same cover gives the sharper inequality `ω(N) ≤ ω(a+1)+ω(i)+Ω(i)`. Here `Ω(i)` counts primes with repetition. A component whose sum has a nontrivial cofactor after removing the `q`-power costs at least one prime factor of `i`. Moreover, the selected prime is the Euler prime and two divides the Euler length while being absent from the support of `N`.

If `i` were prime, writing `t=ω(a+1)` would give `2^t ≤ ω(N) ≤ t+2`. The repository's five-prime lower bound would force `t≥3`, where `2^t > t+2`. A nonspecial index is even and at least six. Thus every component index is composite. The general estimate is `OddPerfect.euler_prime_count_le_length_and_index_factors`; `OddPerfect.exists_proper_prime_dvd_component_index` supplies a proper prime divisor of the actual quotient. Chen and Chen exclude many more Euler index factorization patterns in Corollary 1.4. The Lean proof here covers the prime case using the local five-prime bound. [Chen and Chen, *On the index of an odd perfect number* (2014)](https://doi.org/10.4064/cm136-1-4)

## Small squares and squarefree indices

`OddPerfect.prime_square_index_not_dvd_length` proves that a prime-square Euler index `p²` requires `p ∤ a+1`. Otherwise the prime `p` would be counted twice in the support cover, giving `ω(N) ≤ ω(a+1)+2`, which the five-prime and primitive-order bounds exclude. For `p−1` a power of two, `OddPerfect.component_index_ne_fermat_prime_square` excludes `p²` altogether. An exceptional source forces `p ∣ q−1`, so `p ∤ σ(q^a)` and the exponent of `p` in the cofactor equals two. Its sum `p²+p+1` must be a power of the Euler prime, ruling out `p ≡ 1 (mod 4)`. The remaining Fermat-type case `p=3` forces `q=13`. Each nonspecial component would then have prime length dividing twelve and odd length, so every nonspecial exponent would be two. This contradicts Steuerwald's theorem.

For a squarefree Euler index all of whose prime factors have `p−1` a power of two, `OddPerfect.squarefree_fermat_index_support_dvd_length` puts each such `p` in the Euler length. In an even-exponent component sum contributing `p`, its odd length forces the base to be one modulo `p`, hence `p` divides that length. A primitive order-`p` target cannot be another index prime and so must be `q`. This gives `q ≡ 1 (mod p)`. The index contains a single `p`, while the cofactor square contains at least two, so `p ∣ σ(q^a)` and `p ∣ a+1`. The support cover now gives at least three distinct primes in any such index. In particular it excludes fifteen.

`OddPerfect.euler_component_index_ge_twenty_one` proves both `21 ≤ σ(m²)/q^a` and `21q^a < 2m²` for the Euler component. Stronger general Euler-index bounds appear in the literature. [Broughan, Delbourgo and Zhou, *Improving the Chen and Chen result for odd perfect numbers*](https://kevinbroughan.nz/papers/chenchenformatted.pdf)

## Excluding every prime-square index

`OddPerfect.divisor_sum_component_index_ne_prime_square` in `OddPerfect/PrimeSquareIndex.lean` excludes `p²` for an arbitrary prime `p`, extending the Fermat-prime case. This exclusion is among the published Chen–Chen index restrictions. [Chen and Chen, introduction](https://doi.org/10.4064/cm136-1-4)

Suppose an index equals `p²`. The case `p=2` contradicts the return equation modulo four. Otherwise the index is odd, so the selected component is Euler. With `t=ω(a+1)`, the support bounds give `2^t ≤ ω(N) ≤ t+3` and hence `ω(N)≤5`. At most two cofactor components can have outgoing sums divisible by `p`, since their product divides `q^a p²` and `q≠p`. With at least five prime bases in `N`, one can select a source `ℓ` outside those two bases and `{p,q}`. Its even-exponent component feeds only `q` and receives its full prime power back.

The reciprocal lemma gives `ℓ ∣ a+1`, and `τ(a+1)≤5`. As `a≡1 (mod 4)`, the odd part of `a+1` has at most two divisors and must be `ℓ` itself. Hence `a+1=2ℓ`. The reciprocal lemma now forces the even exponent at `ℓ` to be exactly two. Write `ℓ²+ℓ+1=q^β`. This integer lies strictly between consecutive squares, so `β` is odd. The order of `q` modulo `ℓ` divides `β`. If that order exceeds one, it also divides `2ℓ` through the returning divisor sum. It divides `ℓ−1`, so it divides two, contrary to its oddness. Thus `q≡1 (mod ℓ)`. Lifting the exponent gives `v_ℓ(σ(q^a))=v_ℓ(a+1)=1`, contradicting the full return of `ℓ²`. This final obstruction is the separate theorem `OddPerfect.not_pure_return_at_twice_source_length` in `OddPerfect/ReciprocalValuation.lean`.

## Two distinct prime factors in the Euler index

`OddPerfect.euler_component_index_ne_semiprime` excludes `i=pr` for distinct odd primes `p,r`. Together with the prime and prime-square exclusions, `OddPerfect.three_le_euler_index_total_prime_count` gives `Ω(σ(m²)/q^a) ≥ 3` in Euler form. The odd index is therefore at least 27 and `27q^a < 2m²`. The multiplicity bound counts repeated primes. The nonspecial index has different parity, and these theorems leave its possible values of the form `2p` open. The published Chen–Chen index factorization restrictions and the Broughan–Delbourgo–Zhou numerical bound go further. [Chen and Chen](https://doi.org/10.4064/cm136-1-4) · [Broughan, Delbourgo and Zhou](https://kevinbroughan.nz/papers/chenchenformatted.pdf)

To see the exclusion, suppose `i=pr` and write `t=ω(a+1)`. Then `2^t ≤ ω(N) ≤ t+4`, which gives `t≤2` and `ω(N)≤6`. Since `a+1` is twice an odd integer and `τ(a+1)≤6`, its possible forms are `2`, `2ℓ`, and `2ℓ²` for an odd prime `ℓ`. Each of `p,r` occurs in exactly one cofactor component sum. Mark the two source bases along with `p,r,q`. Every support prime outside this set feeds only `q`, receives its whole power back, and divides the Euler length. There is room for at most one such prime. If the marked set has fewer than five distinct members, the five-prime lower bound supplies an outside source and forces `ω(N)=5`. The preceding full-return obstruction at length `2ℓ` rules this out. Thus neither `p` nor `r` is one of the two exceptional sources.

For either `u=p` or `u=r`, the exponent in `m²` exceeds `v_u(σ(q^a))` by one. The reciprocal valuation forces that exponent to be two. Indeed, a larger even exponent requires `u² ∣ a+1`, which would force six support primes and an outside source dividing the same length. That outside source is the unique odd length prime and is distinct from `u`. Consequently `σ(p²)=q^β` and `σ(r²)=q^γ` with positive `β,γ` satisfying `β+γ≤a`.

This excludes length two. At length `2ℓ` or `2ℓ²`, a pure square source at `ℓ` forces `q≡1 (mod ℓ)`. For a pure square source at a different base `u`, the order of `q` modulo `u` forces `ℓ` to divide both its outgoing exponent and `u−1`. Then `σ(u²)≡3 (mod ℓ)`, ruling out `q≡1 (mod ℓ)`. Hence `p,r` cannot include `ℓ`. At length `2ℓ`, both `β` and `γ` are multiples of `ℓ`, exceeding the available sum `a=2ℓ−1`. At length `2ℓ²`, six support primes are necessary, so an outside source exists at `ℓ` and its exponent is two. Its congruence conflicts with the one just obtained from `p`.

## Fermat primes in the index

Here a Fermat prime means a prime `p` such that `p−1` is a power of two. `OddPerfect.exists_index_prime_not_dvd_length_of_squarefree` proves that every squarefree Euler index has a prime divisor outside `a+1`. Indeed, if all its `s` prime divisors lay in the odd part of the length, then `s ≤ t−1` for `t=ω(a+1)`. The support estimate and squarefreeness would give `2^t ≤ ω(N) ≤ t+s ≤ 2t−1`, contrary to `2t ≤ 2^t`. In particular the earlier squarefree support theorem now excludes *every* squarefree Euler index supported on Fermat primes, irrespective of its number of prime divisors.

For an Euler index supported wholly on such primes, `OddPerfect.fermat_supported_index_valuation` gives `p ∣ q−1` and `2v_p(m) = v_p(a+1)+v_p(i)` for each `p ∣ i`. In addition, every even-exponent component at a Fermat prime `p` has divisor sum coprime to the index. If an index prime `u` divides its sum, the order of `p` modulo `u` divides the odd component length and the power of two `u−1`. Hence `p≡1 (mod u)`, which is incompatible with `p−1` being a power of two. In particular when `p≡1 (mod 4)`, purity of the component and `q≡1 (mod 4)` force `4 ∣ 2v_p(m)`. The result is `OddPerfect.fermat_supported_component_four_dvd_exponent`.

`OddPerfect.euler_component_index_ne_fermat_prime_cube` excludes every cube `p³` of a Fermat prime. The Euler length has at most six divisors, the support of `N` at most six primes, and the index valuation forces `p ∣ a+1`. If `p=3`, then `v_3(a+1)≤2` since `54` has eight divisors. Parity forces exponent four at 3. Its divisor sum is `121`, forcing `q=11` in contradiction to `3 ∣ q−1`. If `p>3`, the proved eight-prime lower bound for odd perfect numbers coprime to three gives `3 ∣ N`. The component at 3 cannot send to `p` since its nontrivial order would be both odd and a divisor of `p−1`. It therefore feeds only `q` and receives its entire power back. The reciprocal lemma gives `3 ∣ a+1`, leaving distinct odd prime divisors `3,p` in a length whose odd part has at most three divisors.

`OddPerfect.euler_component_index_ne_fermat_square_times_prime` also excludes `p²r` for distinct Fermat primes `p,r`. Now the length has at most eight divisors. The valuation at `r` gives `r ∣ a+1`, while `v_p(a+1)` is even. If it were positive, `2p²r` with twelve divisors would divide the length. Thus `p` has exponent two in the cofactor, contradicting the preceding multiple-of-four condition whenever `p>3`. At `p=3` its pure sum is thirteen, forcing `q=13` and `r ∣ 12`, impossible for an odd prime distinct from three. Removing `27` and `45` from the odd integers with `Ω(i)≥3` gives the bound `63q^a < 2m²` and `i≥63` in `OddPerfect.euler_component_index_ge_sixty_three`.
