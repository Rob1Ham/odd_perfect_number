# Counting the odd valuations of the Euler index

Let $O(n)=\{r:r\text{ prime},\ v_r(n)\text{ odd}\}$. For an odd perfect number $N=q^am^2$ in Euler form put $d=(a+1)/2$, $I=\sigma(m^2)/q^a$ and $B=(q^d+1)/2$. Write $\omega$ for distinct prime divisors and $\Omega$ for prime divisors counted with multiplicity. The checked inequalities are
$$\omega(d)+[B\text{ is nonsquare}]\le |O(I)|,\qquad \Omega(d)\le2|O(I)|.$$
Here the bracket denotes zero or one. In particular a square times at most one prime as index, with $a>1$, forces $d=s$ or $s^2$ for a prime $s$. The square case additionally requires $s\mid q-1$. For example, the half-length $d=17\cdot41$ already forces two distinct primes of odd valuation in $I$ for every Euler prime, even though both length primes are one modulo eight. ## Geometric sums at a prime-power base

For $b=p^u\equiv1\pmod4$ with $p$ prime and $u>0$, every geometric sum $G(b,n)=1+b+\cdots+b^{n-1}$ with $n>1$ is nonsquare. This includes $p\equiv3\pmod4$ with even $u$. The statement at prime bases is Hirakawa's Theorem 2.3. At all positive prime-power bases in the indicated residue class it also follows from Ljunggren's wider square classification for $n>2$ and direct inspection at $n=2$. The local argument uses the quadratic-order method. [Prime-power proof](../OddPerfect/GeometricPrimePower.lean) · [Hirakawa, Theorem 2.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.17084v3) · [Ljunggren, Norsk Matematisk Tidsskrift 25 (1943), Theorem 1](https://msleziak.com/temp/Ljunggren.pdf)

Set $D=1-b$. If $G(b,n)=y^2$, an element $z=1+y\sqrt D$ has norm $b^n$. Choose $z$ or its conjugate so that $p$ does not divide the sum of its real and imaginary coefficients. Such an orientation exists because $p$ is odd and the real coefficient is one. The entire $p^u$ then divides the difference between the coefficients, allowing integral division by $1+\sqrt D$. If the quotient had coefficient sum divisible by $p$, the original element would have had this property too. Division thus iterates $n$ times. The norm-one quotient is $1$ or $-1$, which contradicts the coordinate calculation for $(1+\sqrt D)^n$. [Coordinate lemmas](../OddPerfect/GeometricNonSquare.lean)

## How prime factors of the length contribute

Split $G(b,d)$ into mutually coprime blocks $G(b^D,s^k)$ indexed by $s^k\parallel d$, adjoining the primes in descending order. The base of every new block is again a prime power one modulo four, so every block is nonsquare and supplies its own odd-valuation prime. This proves the bound by $\omega(d)$. The coprime factors $G(q,d)$ and $B$ have exactly the same combined odd-valuation support as $I$ by $m^2=G(q,d)BI$. [Coprime geometric blocks](../OddPerfect/GeometricSquareClass.lean) · [Support bookkeeping](../OddPerfect/HalfLengthSupport.lean)

To count repetitions of an odd prime $s$, group two copies at a time. Any common prime of the previously accumulated $G(b,s^j)$ and the next $G(b^{s^j},s^2)$ divides $s^2$, so the only candidate is $s$. Each new block is nonsquare, but its $s$-valuation is even. It is exactly two when $s\mid b^{s^j}-1$ by lifting the exponent and zero otherwise by the Frobenius identity in $\mathbb F_s$. Hence the odd-valuation supports of the accumulated and new blocks are disjoint. This gives $k\le2|O(G(b,s^k))|$. Summing over the mutually coprime blocks proves the $\Omega(d)$ bound. For a prime power $d=s^k$ with $s\nmid q-1$, adding even a single copy gives a coprime block and one fresh odd-valuation prime. This gives $k\le |O(G(q,s^k))|$ and the extra restriction in the $s^2$ case.
